Dogs are waiting three times longer

Austin Animal Center's live release rate has sat between 96 and 97 percent since 2016. Underneath it, the median dog's wait for adoption went from a week to three.

Beck Lyons  ·  October 2026  ·  173,812 intake records, 2014 to 2024

The median dog adopted from Austin Animal Center in 2018 had been there 7 days. In 2023 that figure was 26. In 2024 it was 20.

Over the same stretch, the live release rate went from 97.6 percent to 96.0. That is the number shelters are judged on, the one that turns up in budget hearings and local coverage, and on its own it says nothing happened.

The explanation I reached for first was wrong

Austin's outcome mix has changed a lot. Return to owner fell from 19.8 percent of dog and cat outcomes in 2016 to 7.4 percent in 2024. Transfers to partner groups dropped from 28.6 to 22.7. Adoption picked up the difference, rising from 48 percent to 65.

Those three outcomes take very different amounts of time. A dog returned to its owner is usually gone in a day or two. A transfer takes about four days. An adoption takes weeks. So a shelter doing proportionally more adoptions will show a longer average stay without anything at all having slowed down, which is a boring explanation and a very plausible one.

It is not what happened here. When you hold the outcome type fixed and look only at dogs that were adopted, the wait still tripled. The mix shifted and adoption itself got slower, and the second one is doing the work.

That pattern is what makes me believe it. A building that is simply overwhelmed slows down at every exit. Here the owner reclaim desk is as quick as it ever was and the transfer pipeline is quicker. The delay sits in one place.

The tail moved further than the middle. The slowest tenth of dog adoptions took 64 days in 2018 and 121 days in 2024. Those are the animals that occupy a kennel for four months, and there are more of them than there used to be.

Why the headline metric cannot show this

Live release rate is a ratio of outcomes: how many animals left alive, over how many left at all. It is a good measure of whether a shelter is killing animals for space, which is the thing it was designed to catch, and by that measure Austin is doing well and has been for a decade.

It contains no notion of time. A dog adopted on day 3 and a dog adopted on day 120 are the same single point in the numerator. You could double every length of stay in the building and the rate would not move.

What I would put next to it

Median days to adoption, reported separately for dogs and cats, with the 90th percentile next to it.

Splitting by species is not a technicality. Pooled, the two trends partly cancel: cats are slow and flat, dogs are fast and rising, and the combined line understates what happened to dogs while implying something changed for cats. The species split is the finding, not a cut of it.

The 90th percentile matters because capacity is consumed by the tail. A shelter with a 20 day median and a 121 day 90th percentile is housing a long-stay population that a median alone will never describe.

If I were handed this at the shelter, the question I would want answered next is which dogs are waiting. The obvious candidates are size and behavioural history, and the national pattern since 2021 has been large dogs moving slowly. I could not test that here, for reasons below.

Can you see a long stay coming?

I could not test size, but I could ask a narrower question. Using only what the shelter knows on the day an animal arrives, can you tell which dogs and cats will still be there a month later? If you can, foster outreach and rescue conversations can start on day one instead of day thirty.

So I trained a model to do that on four years of history and tested it on the most recent year in the data, April 2024 to April 2025. In that year, 30.5 percent of dogs and cats stayed 30 days or more. Among the tenth of animals the model scored highest, 58 percent did.

Its AUC on that year is 0.72, against 0.63 for a simple logistic regression. That is useful rather than impressive. It is a ranking of which animals to look at first, not a forecast for any one of them, and four in ten of its highest-scored animals still left within a month. Taking out community cats, who leave within days by design and so are easy to get right, barely moves it.

Age was the strongest signal by a wide margin, then breed, then whether the animal had been spayed or neutered. That last one has a plain explanation: an intact animal needs surgery before it can go home. Breed is the one I would be most careful with. It reflects how adopters choose as much as anything about the dog, biases included, which is one more reason a score like this should only ever be used to start outreach sooner, never to decide which animals get care.

Two things nearly went wrong. The first version ranked animals well but predicted the long-stay rate of the years it learned from, about three points low, because stays had grown longer since. It is now recalibrated against the most recent six months it is allowed to see.

The second was sex, which first came out as the third strongest predictor. Animals recorded as Unknown almost never stayed long, and that is exactly what it looks like when a field gets filled in after the fact. It turned out to be real: half of them were community cats going through the shelter-neuter-return program, and most of the rest went straight to partner groups, many of them very young litters. They were animals nobody could examine on arrival. I dropped sex anyway, because once spay/neuter status was in it added nothing.

What this does not tell you

  • Why it happened. The feed carries breed and colour but no weight, size class, or behavioural flag, so the most likely explanation is also the one I cannot check.
  • The break is 2023, not 2020. Intake volume halved in 2020 and the pandemic disrupted everything, but dog adoption stays sat at 11 to 13 days through 2022 before jumping. Whatever this is, it arrived three years after the obvious candidate.
  • One shelter in one city. Austin is unusually well resourced and unusually committed to a high live release rate. Nothing here generalises without checking.
  • No control group and no intervention. This is a description of a trend, not a causal claim about anything the shelter did.
  • 1,724 records were set aside. 940 outcomes had no matching intake, usually animals admitted before the published range begins, and 784 carried an outcome dated before their intake. That is under one percent of the file. They are listed with reasons in the project's data quality table rather than dropped silently.
  • The model looks backward. The city's feed stops on 4 May 2025, so there is no live list of animals to score. It shows what would have been possible, not what any shelter did with it.
  • The window is 2014 to 2024. The published data starts in October 2013 and ends on 4 May 2025, so both edge years are partial and neither appears in any trend above.

How this was built

Austin publishes two files: one row per intake, one row per outcome. Neither carries a stay identifier, and animals come back, so joining them on the animal's ID alone produces a cross product. One dog in this data has been through the shelter 33 times.

The models pair the nth outcome with the nth intake for each animal, which is correct unless the feed is missing a record in the middle of an animal's history, in which case everything after it shifts by one. That failure shows up as an outcome dated before its intake, so those rows are flagged, excluded, and counted rather than trusted.

The long-stay model is gradient boosting in scikit-learn. It uses only what is known on the day of intake, leaves a 30-day gap between the data it learns from and the data it is tested on, and is re-tested month by month over the final year as if it had been running at the time. Every pull request also runs it on synthetic data where length of stay is random, and the build fails if it finds a pattern there, since that would mean the answer was leaking into the inputs.

Pipeline is dbt on DuckDB, 173,812 intakes and 173,775 outcomes, 54 warehouse tests and 28 more on the model's outputs, rebuilt daily by a scheduled job. The code, the metric definitions, and the generated documentation are at github.com/blyons044/shelter-analytics and the model docs.
